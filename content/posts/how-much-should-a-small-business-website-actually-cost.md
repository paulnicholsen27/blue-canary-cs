This is one of those questions where the honest answer is "it depends," which is deeply unsatisfying when you're trying to budget for it. So let's do better than that. Here's what actually drives the price, what you should expect to pay at different levels, and how to avoid the two most common ways small business owners overpay.

## Why the range is so wide

Search "website cost for small business" and you'll find quotes anywhere from $200 to $20,000, which is about as useful as being told a car costs "somewhere between a bicycle and a private jet." The range is wide because "a website" isn't one product. A five-page site that lists your hours and services is a completely different job from a booking system, an online store, or a site that needs to integrate with your existing scheduling software.

Instead of asking "how much does a website cost," the more useful question is "how much does the specific thing I need cost," so let's break it down that way.

## DIY builders: roughly $10 to $50 a month

Squarespace, Wix, and similar tools let you build something yourself for the price of a subscription. This is a reasonable choice if you're comfortable with a bit of a learning curve, have the time to actually sit down and build it, and your needs are simple.

The catch that doesn't show up in the marketing is time. What looks like a quick weekend project often turns into several weekends, and if you're a business owner already working long days, that's a real cost even though no invoice shows up for it. It's also common to end up with a site that technically works but doesn't quite look professional, because design is its own skill separate from knowing how to use the software.

## Freelancer or small studio: roughly $1,000 to $5,000

This is where most small businesses land, and for good reason. You get a site built specifically for your business, by someone who (hopefully) understands both design and the practical stuff like mobile responsiveness and basic SEO, without agency-level overhead.

The range within this tier depends heavily on scope. A clean five-page informational site sits at the lower end. Adding e-commerce, a booking system, custom animations, or a blog that needs ongoing content structure pushes it toward the higher end. Be wary of quotes far below this range, since they often mean a template with your logo dropped on it and little else, or corners cut on things like mobile optimization that you won't notice until customers start complaining.

## Agency: roughly $5,000 to $20,000+

Agencies bring more hands: strategists, designers, developers, sometimes copywriters, all working together. This tends to make sense for businesses with more complex needs, multiple locations, or brand requirements that go beyond a single site.

For most small, single-location businesses, this tier is more firepower than the job actually calls for. You're not wrong to consider it, but it's worth asking directly what an agency team provides that a skilled freelancer or small studio doesn't, for your specific situation.

## The cost nobody mentions upfront: maintenance

Here's the part that catches people off guard. A website isn't a one-time purchase like a sign for your storefront. Domains need renewing, hosting needs paying, plugins and software need updating, and content needs the occasional refresh. Budget somewhere in the range of $10 to $50 a month for hosting and basic upkeep, more if you want someone actively managing updates and small changes for you.

Skipping this is exactly how a perfectly good website from three years ago turns into the outdated, slow-loading liability we talked about in an earlier post. Cheap upfront and expensive later beats expensive upfront and neglected, but free upfront and neglected is the worst of both.

## Two ways small business owners overpay

**Paying for things you don't need yet.** A ten-page site with a blog, a members' area, and multilingual support sounds impressive, but if you're a local bakery, most of that won't get used and all of it costs money to build and maintain. Start with what actually serves your customers today and add on later if it's genuinely needed.

**Choosing based on price alone.** The cheapest quote often becomes the most expensive site once you factor in the redesign you'll need in a year because it doesn't work properly, isn't mobile-friendly, or nobody can find it in search. Price matters, but "what am I actually getting for this price" matters more.

## So what should you actually budget?

For most small, local businesses that need a clean, professional site with clear information, mobile-friendly design, and basic local SEO, somewhere in the $1,500 to $4,000 range for the build, plus a modest monthly amount for hosting and upkeep, covers it without over- or under-building. Your specific number depends on what you're selling and how customers need to interact with your site, but that's a reasonable starting point for a conversation.
